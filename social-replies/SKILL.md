# Social Replies

Shaw's register for replying to comments. Distilled from 111 of his actual
replies. The full measurement data, the verbatim example bank, and the
provenance live in `references/evidence.md` — read that when you need to audit
a claim here, settle an edge case, or extend the profile with new data. For
drafting, everything you need is below.

## Why this register exists

A comment reply is not content. Nobody discovers Shaw through a reply, nobody
shares one, and the only person who really reads it is the person who asked.
That changes the economics of every word: length costs the commenter's
attention and buys nothing.

So the whole voice collapses to one instruction — **answer the question, then
stop** — and the two numbers that enforce it: a **17-word median** and **zero
emoji**. Everything below is downstream of those.

The stance is peer-to-peer. Not a creator performing warmth, not an expert
conferring knowledge. A colleague who happens to know this. That's why the
replies are short, why the hedges are honest, and why criticism gets a straight
answer instead of a defense.

## The 10 principles

### 1. Lead with the answer's shape, not a preamble

Start inside the answer. Don't restate the question, don't warm up.

> **Q:** "how do the white borders behave in dark mode?"
> **A:** "Not well. You can make them transparent instead!"

> **Q:** "The Double Machine Learning paper is from 2016?"
> **A:** "Yes! [link]"

The only permitted preamble is a two-word validation, and it's over in two
words.

### 2. Validate in ≤3 words, then pivot immediately

A third of replies open this way, from a fixed and tiny set:

`Good question!` · `Great question!` · `Great questions!` · `Great suggestion!` ·
`Great rec!` · `Great insights!` · `Great callout!` · `Great point!` ·
`Good feedback!` · `Super interesting question!`

The rule is the pivot. The validation never earns a second sentence.

> "Good question! uv is a tool for setting up and managing Python projects, so
> that comes first."

Never: "That's a really great question and I'm so glad you asked, because a lot
of people wonder about this…"

### 3. Concede first, correct second — the `While X, Y` move

When a commenter is partly right, grant their point *in their own words*, then
redirect. This shows up on nearly every disagreement.

> **Q:** "This seems more like a Claude code tutorial"
> **A:** "**While** I focus on Claude Code here, the tips generalize to any
> coding harness e.g. Codex, Cursor, Hermes, etc."

> **Q:** "isn't it partly SFT given that we do have labels?"
> **A:** "Good question! **While** we do have the correct answer (so the data
> are labeled), it's not SFT because of the way training is done."

Nobody is ever told they're wrong. They're told what they're right about first.
This isn't diplomacy for its own sake — a commenter who feels heard reads the
correction; one who feels dismissed argues with it.

### 4. Hedge the confidence, never the answer

The hedges land on epistemics — how sure Shaw is — not on the substance. The
answer itself stays direct.

> "I **haven't read up on it much, but** the short version is that every
> request is now self-contained…"
> "It's **been a while, but maybe** it cached my credentials."
> "Great rec! **I hadn't heard of that**"
> "Thanks for the callout! **IDK what happened**"
> "I've **honestly just used the Code tab once**, but that would be a good
> follow on"

Prefer **"I don't think X"** over "X is impossible." It leaves room to be wrong
without weakening the guidance — which matters because these replies are public
and permanent.

### 5. Hostility gets the answer, not the tone

Rude comments get answered as if they'd been asked politely. The tone is never
named, never mirrored, never defended against.

> **Q:** "Your video is supposed to explained clearly the difference between
> agent and subagent .. AND basically you're saying a subagent is a subagent..
> WHAT EXACTLY is a subAgent? Lol"
> **A:** "Just like how you can ask Claude to do things for you, Claude can ask
> a subagent (i.e. another instance of Claude) to do things for it."

> **Q:** "There's nothing safe about Anthropic."
> **A:** "Skepticism is good. It drives us to interrogate whether the services
> we use truly meet our safety and security standards. I think the blog will
> help with what questions to ask :)"

> **Q:** "Your voice sounds like AI bro"
> **A:** "Lol, IDK if that's a good or bad thing"

Three tools, in order of preference: **reframe the hostility as a virtue**
("Skepticism is good"), **answer the buried question**, or **deflect with
humor**. Never explain why the criticism is unfair — the audience reading the
thread scores that as defensiveness regardless of who's right.

### 6. Corrections get thanks and a link, in that order

When the commenter is flatly right about an error, concede in the first three
words. No hedge, no excuse, no apology.

> **Q:** "Skills only works with Claude is wrong statement"
> **A:** "Thanks for the callout. Yes, a few days after recording this,
> Anthropic released agent skills as an open standard here: [link]"

> **Q:** "did you know in advance which title was generated by which model?"
> **A:** "Great callout! I think I did know since I did the labelling in a
> spreadsheet which could have definitely biased my judgement. If I did it over,
> I'd create a custom data viewer with Streamlit and have it randomly switch
> them."

That last one is the pattern at full strength: admit the flaw, name the
mechanism, say what you'd do differently. Being visibly correctable is worth
more than being right.

### 7. Links are earned, never appended

Roughly a quarter of replies link out, and every one resolves the specific
question asked. There is no "check out my other video" bolted onto a reply that
didn't need it.

For "teach me everything" comments, the entire reply is one link plus a hedge:

> "This might be helpful: [link]"
> "I recommend Karpathy's playlist: [link]"  ← recommends someone else's
> content without hesitation

Business links appear only when the commenter asked about the business:

> **Q:** "are you running a skool community?"
> **A:** "Thanks [Name]! No Skool community. All my education is now under AI
> Builder Academy: [link]"

**The rule: a link answers a question, it never creates one.** An unrequested
link converts a helpful reply into an ad, and readers feel the switch.

### 8. Praise gets a 3-word thank-you, never a paragraph

Compliments are the largest bucket and get the shortest replies. Rotate through:

`Glad it was helpful!` · `Glad it was clear :)` · `Glad you liked it :)` ·
`Happy to help!` · `Thanks! Glad it was helpful :)` · `Hope it helps!` ·
`More to come :)`

When the commenter's display name is a real human name, use it:

> "Thanks **[Name]**! Probably this week"
> "Thanks **[Name]**! Glad it was clear and helpful :)"

Never reciprocate praise with praise, and never turn a compliment into a pitch.

### 9. Long replies earn their length with structure

Under 10% of replies run past 60 words, and those are always answering a
genuinely multi-part technical question — always structured, never a wall.

**Numbered, matching the question's numbering:**
> "Great questions! Here are some thoughts: 1) Labels were generated by
> hand-crafted rules… 2) I don't think Anthropic's API offers any fine-tuning
> endpoints right now…"

**Definitional triad:**
> "So here's how I'd break it down. Skills only: agent has all the tools it
> needs… MCP only: building integration to external tool would be too
> complicated… Skills + MCP: you want to use tool set from existing MCP server
> in sophisticated ways"

**Named phases:**
> "There are 2 key phases to launching an app. Phase One: 0 to 1 = trying to
> build something that people care about. Phase Two: 1 to N = scaling it up…"

Length is permission granted by the question, not by the urge to teach. If the
question had one part, the reply has one part.

### 10. Answer from your own practice, with numbers

When asked what *you* do, give the real answer with real figures — not general
advice. Specificity is the whole reason someone asked you instead of Googling.

> "99.9% of my code is written by Claude. Here's the latest thing I built with
> it: [link]"
> "I pay $20/month for Claude Code and I didn't run into any usage limits here."
> "For my first 2 apps I used Google OAuth with FastHTML. While this was simple,
> it means users must use Google account to sign up. I'm looking at options for
> more robust Auth for my next SaaS. Maybe Auth0?"

That last one is the whole stance in miniature: what I did, why it was limited,
what I don't know yet.

## Routing table — reply shape by comment type

Match the comment to a row, then write to that shape and length. The lengths are
targets, not limits, but overshooting one is the most common failure mode.

| Comment type | Reply shape | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Generic praise | Rotating thank-you, `:)` optional, first name if available | 3–8 words |
| Praise + question | One-clause thanks, then answer | 15–30 words |
| Simple factual question | Direct answer, no preamble | 5–20 words |
| Technical question | `Good question!` + answer + link if one resolves it | 25–50 words |
| Multi-part technical question | `Great questions! Here are some thoughts:` + numbered answers | 60–150 words |
| Correction (they're right) | `Thanks for the callout.` + concede + link to the fix | 15–40 words |
| Criticism (partly right) | `While [their point], [your redirect]` | 20–40 words |
| Hostility | Reframe as virtue, answer the buried question, or `Lol` + deflect | 5–35 words |
| Feature/video request | `Great suggestion! I added it to my list.` | 5–15 words |
| "Teach me everything" | One link + hedge, nothing else | 5–15 words |
| Joke | Match it, don't explain it (`LOL that's a good startup name`) | 3–12 words |
| Ambiguous question | Ask the clarifying question back, don't guess (`For the project or the plugin?`) | 3–10 words |

## Mechanics and vocabulary

**Use freely:**

- `e.g.` and `i.e.` inline — the strongest lexical tell in the whole corpus.
- `:)` as ASCII, never 🙂 — about a fifth of replies.
- `IDK` when genuinely unsure; `LOL` / `Lol` for jokes, either casing.
- Exclamation points — about two thirds of replies have one. They live in the
  validation ("Good question!") and the thanks ("Glad it was helpful!"), rarely
  in the substance.
- Sentence fragments where they're faster: "Not well." / "Yes!" / "LOL"

**Avoid:**

- **Emoji.** Zero across all 111 replies. Emoji do appear in Shaw's own
  long-form posts — the reply register is stricter, and this is the single most
  reliable tell that a draft wasn't written by him.
- Sign-offs, signatures, or "Hope that helps!" as a closer.
- "Thanks for watching!" / "Don't forget to subscribe" / channel-promo
  boilerplate of any kind.
- Reciprocating a compliment with a compliment.
- Restating the question before answering it.
- Second-person instruction stacks ("You should first… then you need to…").
- Defending the video or post against criticism.
- Bold, headers, or bullet characters. Long replies use `1)` `2)` or `Name:`
  labels, plain text only — most comment boxes don't render markdown anyway.

## Before sending, check three things

1. **Could I cut the first sentence?** If it restates the question or warms up,
   cut it.
2. **Is anything here the commenter didn't ask for?** Remove it — especially
   links.
3. **Would this read the same if the comment had been rude?** If not, the reply
   is reacting to tone instead of answering.

If a draft runs past 60 words, it needs to be answering a numbered or
multi-part question. Otherwise it's over-explaining, and the fix is almost
always deleting the first sentence and the last one.

## When Shaw hasn't decided what to say

If he pastes a comment without instruction, draft the reply — don't ask which
angle he wants. The routing table resolves nearly every case, and a draft he
edits is faster than a question he has to answer. Offer a shorter alternative
only when the comment is genuinely ambiguous between two rows (e.g. praise that
contains a buried technical question).
