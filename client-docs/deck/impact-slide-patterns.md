# Impact slide patterns

The impact slide lives or dies on whether the proof is real and relevant. This file covers turning raw research into honest, persuasive slide content.

## Segment by the audience in the room

Group the impact by the actual roles the client named — most often three columns:

- **Executives** — strategic, time-back, decision-quality wins.
- **Non-technical champions** — knowledge-work automation (reports, content, inbox/calendar).
- **Technical champions / engineers** — engineering-leverage wins (data, code review, understanding systems).

Each buyer should see their own world in one column. Match real case studies to the segment where they fit.

## Case study vs Example — tag everything

This distinction is the integrity of the slide. Tag each item:

- **Case study** — a real result from a real person or engagement. Mark it visibly (a small colored tag). Attribute by name only if the client approved it (published testimonials are safe); otherwise de-personalize ("a marketing leader", "an executive assistant"). Use Shaw's preference for the specific client.
- **Example** — an illustrative capability you can demonstrate but isn't yet tied to a named client (e.g. ask data questions in plain English; secure-code review on every PR; understand a legacy service in minutes). Honest framing: these are what's *possible*, shown live, not past results.

Never let an example masquerade as a proven result. If a slide title implies all items are "proven," but some are examples, soften the title or move the examples.

## Before → after is the strongest frame

The most persuasive impact line pairs the old cost with the new. Intake forms hold the "before" number; check-in/results notes hold the "after." Examples this skill has produced from real data:

- "Automated a CEO's cross-region cashflow summary across four currencies, taking a recurring 1-2 hour task down to minutes."
- "Newsletter that took 10 hours a week, rebuilt with three skills, now about 1 hour."
- "A 3-week financial analysis turned into a 1-hour task; email roughly 50% faster."
- "Calendar and inbox triage cut from 45 minutes a day to about 5."

Phrase impacts compactly, often as "X → minutes" or "10 hrs/wk → 1 hr." Keep the metric vocabulary consistent across the deck (e.g. Volume, Quality, Cost, Time) so the argument reads as one piece.

## Sourcing notes

- Prefer **published, named testimonials** for anything you'll attribute — they're client-approved.
- **Engagement Results sections** and **check-in notes** give specific numbers but are often unsigned — fine for de-personalized "what people built," careful with names.
- **Intake-form sheets** are the only reliable source for the *before* duration. Read the specific person's row; the "how much time did you spend" column is what you want.
- Always keep a source link for each number so Shaw can verify and so you can cite.

## Watch-outs

- Some clients must stay anonymous even when others are named — respect per-person rules.
- Don't overclaim: if you only have a verified number for one segment, say so and mark the others as examples rather than inventing figures.
- Flag gaps to Shaw ("technical column has no signed case study yet — using examples") rather than papering over them.
