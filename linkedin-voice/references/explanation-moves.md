# Explanation moves (carried over from Shaw's live calls)

How Shaw explains things out loud, distilled from ten call transcripts (workshops, sales calls, client check-ins — June–July 2026). These translate directly into written explanations:

- **Count it out loud, then unpack.** Announce the shape before the content: "it comes down to three things", "The way I see it, there are 2 ways to do this...", "It's two different things." The reader gets the container first; then each item gets unpacked in order.
- **Explain through workplace analogies — and flag them.** The master frame is the intern/new hire, extended with first-day-on-the-job detail ("imagine you hire an intern but don't give them access to Jira — they're not going to be as helpful as they could be"). Coin others from everyday work and life, never from technical reference points: a context window = "10 textbooks worth of text", a sandbox = "a computer within your computer", AI overspend = "an ignorance tax". It's fine to tag the device ("to use an analogy").
- **Concrete first, principle after.** Show the real thing — the story, the number, the failure — then name the concept it illustrates. Defer abstraction explicitly rather than front-loading a definition. Even mistakes convert to material: "mistakes are good because it gives us an opportunity to give feedback and iterate."
- **Close every explanation with a punch line.** A long explanatory passage ends in a one-line compression: "That's the punch line." / "It's just faster, better." / "It's just a smarter version of chat." When the punch line names the real lesson by negating the expected one, it's the reframe couplet (see Signature sentence-level moves in SKILL.md) — the punch line is the general case.
- **Tiered advice: the proper way vs. the scrappy way.** Advice never comes as one answer. Give two paths ranked by effort, then the rule for choosing: "don't jump to the proper way just because it's the proper way", "go after the lowest hanging fruit first", start small and scrappy, you can always refactor later.
- **Cast the reader into a role and do their math.** Name who the reader is in the story ("If you're the champion for your team...") and run their arithmetic for them: "you spend eight hours a week experimenting so the other three PMs don't have to. That 3xes the return."
