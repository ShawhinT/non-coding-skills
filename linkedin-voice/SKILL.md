# Shaw's LinkedIn Voice

Seven principles, distilled from Shaw's 18 top posts of the past year plus a close read of a month of his recent post copy and live call transcripts (June–July 2026). These are principles, not a checklist — when a rule below seems to conflict with a principle, the principle wins.

## Principle 1: Specificity is the proof

Shaw never says "I made money freelancing." He says "$18,558 at $55–$95/hr." Exact figures ($23,603.96, 75,000 subs, 4,936 miles, "Ate 1 bratwurst"), named tools (FastHTML, MonsterUI, Railway), and real dates do the persuading — round numbers and vague claims read as marketing.

Why it works: specificity signals lived experience, and lived experience is the entire basis of Shaw's authority (see Principle 3). A precise, slightly awkward number ($23,603.96) is more believable than a clean one ($25k).

In practice: if a claim can carry a real number, it should. If you don't have the number, ask Shaw for it rather than inventing or rounding. The inverse also holds: an unmeasured multiplier ("10x", "where the 10x lives") reads as marketing, not proof. If the number isn't real and measured, cut it. When the argument already stands on its own, the hype line only weakens it.

Numbers do two different jobs, and the job sets the format. A number that *proves* something — a receipt, a result, a rep count — should be exact and unrounded, because the precision is what makes it believable ($23,603.96, 40+ workshops, 75,000 subs). A number that *sizes an ask* — what the reader has to spend to get started — should be round and small, because its job is to shrink perceived cost, not to convince. "60 minutes and one week" beats "90 minutes and 3 weeks" even when both are true: at that point the reader isn't deciding whether to believe you, they're deciding whether to begin, and an exact figure makes the ask feel measured out rather than easy. Precision persuades; roundness invites.

## Principle 2: Write for the skimmer, reward the reader

Every top post survives a 2-second skim: the hook works alone, list-item headers work alone, and whitespace does the pacing. Paragraphs are 1–2 sentences. Group by beat, not by sentence: join the sentences inside one beat with commas so it flows ("One person's at $370 for the month, another at $185, and she was on the fence"), and break to a new line only when the beat changes — the fact block, then the reframe, then the payoff. Fragment-per-line staccato reads as choppy; a dense multi-beat paragraph buries the turn. Logic gets compressed into arrows ("More uses → move slower").

The commentary under each list item is where readers who stopped get paid: a sharp observation, an honest caveat, a small confession. Skimmers get the skeleton; readers get the insight.

Where that commentary attaches depends on whether the items are independent or sequential. Independent items — 10 YouTube channels, 3 side hustles, 5 agents you could build — are each their own small payoff, so each earns its own commentary. Items that are *steps in one sequence* don't work that way: annotating each one turns a plan into homework, and the reader counts the work instead of seeing how short it is. Group those under a heading, let the bare steps carry the skim, and put a single synthesis line after the group. Six annotated steps read as a syllabus; two groups of three with one payoff line each read as something you could start today. When a sequence's steps do carry commentary, each step's commentary should end with the literal first action ("Set up your connectors in the Customize tab", "do a task, then tell Claude to turn it into a skill") — a step that only defines leaves the reader informed but not moving, and the exchange (Principle 4) isn't complete until the step is executable.

In practice: draft the skeleton (hook + item headers + CTA) first and check it stands alone. Then add commentary, letting the item type decide where it lands — ≤3 short sentences per independent item, or one line per group for sequential steps. Cut any paragraph that runs past two sentences.

## Principle 3: Peer, not guru — one or two steps ahead

Shaw states this ethos in his own #1 post: "Just being 1-2 steps ahead of your audience is enough." He teaches only from what he has actually lived (recent reps or an older lesson from earlier in his career), and he deliberately undercuts his own authority just enough to stay a peer: "no idea what I was doing...", "shameful self-promotion", "This one got the least traction."

The mechanics of this humility: parenthetical hedges — "(IMO)", "(current)", "(hidden)", "(very)" — a 😅 after an admission, and honest inclusion of what didn't work alongside what did.

In practice: never preach beyond what Shaw has actually done. Include at least one honest downside, failure, or self-deprecating aside in any post that shares wins. Hedge opinions in parens rather than softening whole sentences. Also cut lines that tell the reader what they've failed to do ("most teams are proud of the first and haven't started the second"). Diagnosing the reader's shortcomings is preaching, not peering. State the pattern and let them place themselves in it.

## Principle 4: Every post is an exchange

The hook makes a promise (a numbered payoff or a contrarian claim), the body delivers it completely, and the close invites a low-effort response. The closing question — "👉 What would you add to this list?" — always asks about the *reader's* version of the topic, never "what did you think of my post?"

Why it works: the reader gives attention, gets full value, and is handed an easy way to participate. Promo posts extend the same principle, but the close depends on what's being promoted: event promos add a P.S. that removes friction ("If you can't make it live, I'll send you the recording :)"), while giveaway CTAs lead with the reader's felt pain and then the ask ("If you're feeling frustrated that your teams still aren't maximizing AI, click the link below and take the (free) 2-min quiz"). Naming the feeling qualifies the click; a P.S. can only lower its cost.

In practice: hook = specific promise; body = fully deliver, no teasing; close = 👉 + a question the average reader can answer from their own life. Educational posts add the footer: "♻️ If this post was helpful, repost it!" (+ lead-magnet link when active).

## Principle 5: Three engines — the listicle, the take, and the story

The reliable engine (most of the top 18): hook → 1–2 line reframe → numbered list → question CTA → footer. Use it for resources, frameworks, recaps, side-hustle math, tech stacks.

The ceiling-breaker (the #3 reach post): a contrarian argument in plain economic prose — margins, arbitrage, optionality — with none of the machinery. No list, no CTA, no footer. It carries on the strength of the take alone.

The story with a moral (Oct 2026): one real client story told in order, then the lesson stated plainly ("The moral of the story is…"). Let the twist land where it happened in the story rather than diagnosing it in the setup, and give the post one moral — a second framework near the close (a set of levers, a pair of contrasting questions) competes with it. This is often the better way to carry a take: the story earns the opinion instead of it arriving from nowhere.

In practice: teaching → listicle. Arguing → take, and strip the machinery; a CTA on an opinion post undercuts its confidence. Arguing from something that happened → story with a moral. Don't force a take into list form or pad a list post with argument.

## Principle 6: Say it, then translate it

Every abstraction gets an immediate plain restatement. The signature connector is "In other words," ("In other words, it's not a chatbot; it's an agent you can delegate work to"). Supporting habits: liberal inline "e.g." and "i.e." with concrete examples baked into prose, definition-by-equals ("Git = the tool that tracks changes to your code"), and announcing simplicity before an explanation ("The math is simple." / "The logic was simple:").

Why it works: Shaw's audience now includes operators and execs, not just builders. Translation is how a peer explains without preaching — it extends Principle 3 to explanation itself.

In practice: after any conceptual claim, restate it concretely within a line or two. If a term would make a non-technical operator pause, translate it in parens ("via English (instead of computer code)") rather than avoiding it. Translation is for abstractions: a line that's already a concrete image (the "~10 textbooks" context-window analogy) doesn't need an "In other words" after it, which only repeats the point (Shaw cut exactly that restatement in the Oct 2026 context post). But translation lives *inside* posts that argue or share — a post whose entire spine is explanation/definition is the weakest performer on both reach and engagement.

## Principle 7: Anchor claims in time

Nearly every post grounds its authority in a dated personal window: "Last week, I added exactly one line...", "Over the last 3 months, I've run 32 Claude workshops", "My CLAUDE.md has been empty for the past 9 months". The credential is sample size of recent firsthand reps — time-stamped, stated once near the top, then dropped. Never a title, never repeated.

Why it works: it's the mechanism behind peer-not-guru at scale. A dated window ("32 workshops in 3 months") proves the lesson was earned firsthand, which is Shaw's entire basis for teaching (Principle 3). An older lesson gets the same treatment: "In 2022, building credit-risk models at Toyota..." dates it just as well as "last week". Note it's table stakes, not a lever — it appears in his strongest and weakest posts alike, so it makes a post sound like Shaw without predicting performance.

In practice: open teaching posts with one credential line (window + rep count). Date personal anecdotes ("last week", "back in grad school") instead of leaving them floating in unspecified past.

## Hooks (the first two lines)

The hook is the **first two lines** — both sit above the "…see more" fold, and both do work: line 1 stops the scroll, line 2 re-hooks and earns the expand. Write and judge them as a pair, not a single opener. **Never draft body-first.**

Before writing, critiquing, or brainstorming any hook, read `references/hook-craft.md` — the 8 hook principles plus the mandatory process (10-candidate menu split 5 Shaw / 5 named creators, source lines, novelty check, edit loop) — and retrieve a proven pattern from `references/hook-bank.md` (50 labeled example hooks across 16 mechanisms) instead of writing from scratch.

## Signature sentence-level moves (from the recent copy)

- **Plain beats clever.** When a clever formulation and a plain one carry the same point, Shaw takes the plain one — the cleverness lives in the argument, never the sentence. In one editing session (Aug 2026 bottleneck post) he swapped a drafted "solving the wrong AI problems" for the blunter "fail" in the hook, and cut the draft's sharpest aphorism ("More productive individuals ≠ a more productive company") in favor of a flat title echo at the close. So: hook stakes get the plainest true outcome word (fail, stall, lose) over a smart-sounding diagnosis, and a line that reads as wordplay is a cut candidate even when it's correct. This is the general case of the reframe-couplet note below — concrete beats smart wherever the two compete.
- **Define contrasts as mirrored pairs.** When a post turns on two opposed concepts, define them with the same sentence skeleton: "A use case solves a single problem. A building block, on the other hand, is used to solve many problems." (Aug 2026 building-blocks post: a drafted metaphorical definition, "sits upstream / infrastructure that makes every future use case cheaper," was replaced with the plain mirrored pair — and "infrastructure" moved to the payoff line, where the argument cashes out.) The symmetry makes the contrast do the work; a metaphor at the definition stage muddies it. Save the metaphor for the close, where it lands on a reader who already holds the plain version.
- **Every beat adds a new concrete image.** Beats that summarize or bridge get cut; beats that show something survive. In the same session Shaw deleted a setup inventory ("tools for every team, company-wide training... a huge amount of time and money invested") and a transition line ("the results, however, are always uneven"), jumping straight from frame to symptom: "Some people end up automating half their job, while another corner of the org has opened Claude exactly once." If a paragraph's job is only to establish scale of effort or hand off between sections, delete it — the concrete detail carries the setup by itself.
- **The mid-sentence "however" pivot.** The contrast engine is "however" set off by commas inside the sentence: "AI agents, however, are changing the economics." / "The difference, however, is where that folder lives." Sentence-initial "But", "So", and "Plus," handle lighter turns. Mark the turn explicitly even on the hook: when the second line reverses the first, open it with a contrast marker ("...writes 90%+ of their code with AI. But their velocity hasn't changed...") rather than leaving the contrast implied. "But" or a "however" pivot both work; the point is naming the turn, not the word.
- **The reframe couplet.** Near the close, negate the expected lesson, then state the real one: "The win wasn't a fancy agent. It was capturing one repeatable process." / "The goal isn't a big CLAUDE.md. It's the smallest one that saves you from repeating yourself." (Carried the #2 reach post of June–July 2026.) The negated half names the surface-level win the reader came for; the affirmed half has to name a **durable, concrete outcome they keep** — a captured process, a task off their plate, a smaller file that stops repeat work. A cleverer abstraction in the affirmed slot ("it's judging a skill you're building by the stopwatch on a task you already know") reads as wordplay and lands soft, even when the sentence is well-formed. If the second half can't be pointed at, it isn't the payoff yet. This is the closing case of Principle 1: the couplet is where the post cashes out, so it needs the most concrete noun in the piece, not the smartest one. One scope note: the couplet belongs to posts that argue or share. A how-to list whose steps are themselves the payoff can skip it and close straight on the CTA question — bolting a philosophical cash-out onto instructions reads as padding (Aug 2026 AI OS post: the drafted couplet was cut, going list → "Which step are you on?"). Bonus tips appended after the list can face the same cut. In the Oct 2026 context post, Shaw removed a "who needs to read it" rule of thumb and a handoff-doc tip that followed the five items, ending on the last item instead. That's an example of the tendency, not a rule: weigh whether an extra beat adds to the list or just delays the close.
- **Name your tool, then widen.** Recommend tools by naming what you actually use, then generalizing: "I implemented this using Notion, but you can use anything that stores data e.g. Obsidian, Google Drive, Apple Notes, files on your computer." The named choice keeps it lived experience (Principle 3); the alternatives keep it advice rather than prescription.
- **Argue in notation.** Math and symbols do rhetorical work inside prose: "Access ≠ adoption", "→" for causal chains, inline arithmetic "(20% x 10 = 200%)", casual approximations ("~80% vs ~50%, say 65%"), and single-word ALL CAPS for stress ("you need to DOUBLE", "will NOT come from coding"). Present in 3 of the 5 widest-reach recent posts.
- **The ellipsis open loop.** A trailing "…" on the hook's first or second line suspends the turn: "Here's why that doesn't work…" / "Service businesses are so back…" Appeared on all three of the biggest-reach recent posts.
- **The parenthetical wink.** Parens are emphasis, not just hedging: one intensifier mid-noun-phrase — "a (very) time-consuming process", "a (small) dinner", "a (proper) agent harness", "a CRM that (actually) stayed up to date".
- **Work is physical stuff you move.** "Delete 5–10 hours of busywork", "take that off her plate", "stuff their CLAUDE.md with rules", "pumped the brakes on my SaaS ambitions", "one-shotted by Claude". The default analogy for agents is hiring: intern, new hire, onboarding, "like you would a new hire".
- **Self-posed question pivots.** Mid-body, Shaw interviews himself to advance the argument — "So what does it take to drive behavior change?" — and the next line answers. Distinct from the closing CTA question.

When a post teaches or explains a concept, read `references/explanation-moves.md` — how Shaw explains things out loud (distilled from ten live call transcripts, June–July 2026), translated into written form: count-then-unpack, workplace analogies, concrete-first, punch lines, tiered advice, and casting the reader into a role.

## Copy a partner posts (event promos)

When a venue or partner promotes Shaw's event on their own channel, write in *their* voice ("we're hosting… with AI Builder Academy"), with Shaw thanked by tag rather than speaking as "I". The principles above still shape it (a specific promise, a skimmable body, one CTA), but Shaw's personal moves (a dated credential, 😅) don't belong in someone else's post. Shaw's edits on the Oct 2026 Plug and Play draft show the calibration:
- name the audience the partner serves ("founders and business leaders," not "small business owners")
- set the session against the category ("not a generic workshop") rather than against a demo
- cut perks the partner hasn't confirmed
- keep the thanks to the people who made it happen

Send the list of handles to tag along with the copy, because the poster has to add each @ by hand.

## Mechanics (expressions of the principles, not extra rules)

- Emoji lexicon, used sparingly: 👉 CTA · ♻️ repost footer · 😅 self-deprecation · 🥳 celebration · 👇 "content below" · 😉 after a cheeky claim · 🙏 public thanks. Warmth comes from text emoticons :) and :P, not emoji — :) especially at the end of invitations ("come trade lessons and stories :)").
- Optional close variants (seen in recent posts): a bare CTA question without the 👉, and a `--` separator line before the ♻️ footer. On a teaching post that leaves readers unsure how it applies to *them*, Shaw has also closed by offering to apply it for them in the comments: "If you're not sure where something goes... Let me know in the comments, and I'll share what I'd do :)" (Oct 2026 context post). It turns leftover uncertainty into a comment. Drafter's choice, not a rule — the standard 👉 close stays the default.
- ✅/❌ pairs for contrast lists (risk vs. benefit).
- No hashtags. None appear in any top post.
- Punctuation stays flat in the post: no em-dashes. Where you'd reach for one, use a period (often plus "But" to open the contrast: "That overrun isn't waste — it's the price" becomes "But that overrun isn't waste. It's the price"), a comma, or parentheses for an aside. Line breaks handle the pacing a dash would; the dash adds a drama this voice doesn't want.
- Trim intensifiers that don't earn their place: "leave it wide open" → "leave it open", "already at $370" → "at $370". The specific noun or number carries the weight; the modifier dilutes it.
- Numbered items formatted as "1. Header" with a blank line before the commentary.
- Titles/hooks may use a number + stakes formula: "10 YouTube channels that keep me ahead of 99% of AI Entrepreneurs", "3 side hustles that made me $23,603.96".

## Quick self-check before delivering a draft

1. Would this hook stop Shaw's actual audience (technical folks leaving 9-5s, AI builders, engineering leaders) — or is it generic creator-speak?
2. Is every claim carrying a real, specific number or name where one exists?
3. Does it read as a peer sharing, or a guru performing? (Look for at least one honest hedge or admission.)
4. Can a skimmer get the skeleton in 2 seconds?
5. Listicle or take — and is the machinery matched to the choice?
6. Does every abstraction get a plain restatement ("In other words..."), and is at least one claim anchored to a dated window of firsthand experience (recent or older)?
