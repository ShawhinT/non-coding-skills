# Shaw's LinkedIn Voice

Seven principles, distilled from Shaw's 18 top posts of the past year plus a close read of a month of his recent post copy and live call transcripts (June–July 2026). These are principles, not a checklist — when a rule below seems to conflict with a principle, the principle wins.

## Principle 1: Specificity is the proof

Shaw never says "I made money freelancing." He says "$18,558 at $55–$95/hr." Exact figures ($23,603.96, 75,000 subs, 4,936 miles, "Ate 1 bratwurst"), named tools (FastHTML, MonsterUI, Railway), and real dates do the persuading — round numbers and vague claims read as marketing.

Why it works: specificity signals lived experience, and lived experience is the entire basis of Shaw's authority (see Principle 3). A precise, slightly awkward number ($23,603.96) is more believable than a clean one ($25k).

In practice: if a claim can carry a real number, it should. If you don't have the number, ask Shaw for it rather than inventing or rounding. The inverse also holds: an unmeasured multiplier ("10x", "where the 10x lives") reads as marketing, not proof. If the number isn't real and measured, cut it. When the argument already stands on its own, the hype line only weakens it.

## Principle 2: Write for the skimmer, reward the reader

Every top post survives a 2-second skim: the hook works alone, list-item headers work alone, and whitespace does the pacing. Paragraphs are 1–2 sentences. Group by beat, not by sentence: join the sentences inside one beat with commas so it flows ("One person's at $370 for the month, another at $185, and she was on the fence"), and break to a new line only when the beat changes — the fact block, then the reframe, then the payoff. Fragment-per-line staccato reads as choppy; a dense multi-beat paragraph buries the turn. Logic gets compressed into arrows ("More uses → move slower").

The commentary under each list item is where readers who stopped get paid: a sharp observation, an honest caveat, a small confession. Skimmers get the skeleton; readers get the insight.

In practice: draft the skeleton (hook + item headers + CTA) first and check it stands alone. Then add ≤3 short sentences of commentary per item. Cut any paragraph that runs past two sentences.

## Principle 3: Peer, not guru — one or two steps ahead

Shaw states this ethos in his own #1 post: "Just being 1-2 steps ahead of your audience is enough." He teaches only from recent personal experience, and he deliberately undercuts his own authority just enough to stay a peer: "no idea what I was doing...", "shameful self-promotion", "This one got the least traction."

The mechanics of this humility: parenthetical hedges — "(IMO)", "(current)", "(hidden)", "(very)" — a 😅 after an admission, and honest inclusion of what didn't work alongside what did.

In practice: never preach beyond what Shaw has actually done. Include at least one honest downside, failure, or self-deprecating aside in any post that shares wins. Hedge opinions in parens rather than softening whole sentences. Also cut lines that tell the reader what they've failed to do ("most teams are proud of the first and haven't started the second"). Diagnosing the reader's shortcomings is preaching, not peering. State the pattern and let them place themselves in it.

## Principle 4: Every post is an exchange

The hook makes a promise (a numbered payoff or a contrarian claim), the body delivers it completely, and the close invites a low-effort response. The closing question — "👉 What would you add to this list?" — always asks about the *reader's* version of the topic, never "what did you think of my post?"

Why it works: the reader gives attention, gets full value, and is handed an easy way to participate. Promo posts extend the same principle with a P.S. that removes friction ("If you can't make it live, I'll send you the recording :)").

In practice: hook = specific promise; body = fully deliver, no teasing; close = 👉 + a question the average reader can answer from their own life. Educational posts add the footer: "♻️ If this post was helpful, repost it!" (+ lead-magnet link when active).

## Principle 5: Two engines — the listicle and the take

The reliable engine (most of the top 18): hook → 1–2 line reframe → numbered list → question CTA → footer. Use it for resources, frameworks, recaps, side-hustle math, tech stacks.

The ceiling-breaker (the #3 reach post): a contrarian argument in plain economic prose — margins, arbitrage, optionality — with none of the machinery. No list, no CTA, no footer. It carries on the strength of the take alone.

In practice: teaching → listicle. Arguing → take, and strip the machinery; a CTA on an opinion post undercuts its confidence. Don't force a take into list form or pad a list post with argument.

## Principle 6: Say it, then translate it

Every abstraction gets an immediate plain restatement. The signature connector is "In other words," ("In other words, it's not a chatbot; it's an agent you can delegate work to"). Supporting habits: liberal inline "e.g." and "i.e." with concrete examples baked into prose, definition-by-equals ("Git = the tool that tracks changes to your code"), and announcing simplicity before an explanation ("The math is simple." / "The logic was simple:").

Why it works: Shaw's audience now includes operators and execs, not just builders. Translation is how a peer explains without preaching — it extends Principle 3 to explanation itself.

In practice: after any conceptual claim, restate it concretely within a line or two. If a term would make a non-technical operator pause, translate it in parens ("via English (instead of computer code)") rather than avoiding it. But translation lives *inside* posts that argue or share — a post whose entire spine is explanation/definition is the weakest performer on both reach and engagement.

## Principle 7: Anchor claims in time

Nearly every post grounds its authority in a dated personal window: "Last week, I added exactly one line...", "Over the last 3 months, I've run 32 Claude workshops", "My CLAUDE.md has been empty for the past 9 months". The credential is sample size of recent firsthand reps — time-stamped, stated once near the top, then dropped. Never a title, never repeated.

Why it works: it's the mechanism behind peer-not-guru at scale. A dated window ("32 workshops in 3 months") proves the lesson was earned recently, which is Shaw's entire basis for teaching (Principle 3). Note it's table stakes, not a lever — it appears in his strongest and weakest posts alike, so it makes a post sound like Shaw without predicting performance.

In practice: open teaching posts with one credential line (window + rep count). Date personal anecdotes ("last week", "this month") instead of leaving them floating in unspecified past.

## Hook Principles (the first two lines)

The seven principles govern the whole post; these govern the hook specifically, because the hook is a separate craft with its own failure modes. Distilled from a teardown of Shaw's 278 posts (Jul 2025–Jul 2026). The hook is the **first two lines** — both sit above the "…see more" fold, and both do work: line 1 stops the scroll, line 2 re-hooks and earns the expand. Write and judge them as a pair, not a single opener. The full labeled library of 50 example hooks (35 of Shaw's + 15 borrowed from top creators, across 16 mechanisms) lives in `references/hook-bank.md` — **retrieve a proven pattern from there before writing a hook from scratch.**

1. **Promise + stakes in the same breath.** "10 YouTube channels" is nothing; "…that keep me ahead of 99% of AI Entrepreneurs" hit 52k. If the hook names a thing, it must name why to care, in the same line. A bare topic or bare number underperforms.
2. **The first four words decide it.** Open on a concrete subject — I, you, a number, or a named role — never an abstract category. The clearest dead-hook signal in the data is a trend opener ("AI adoption is becoming table stakes…" stalled at 1k impressions). If the first line could open a McKinsey slide, rewrite it.
3. **Name the turn.** The strongest non-list hooks carry an internal reversal: "safe… until it isn't", "The question isn't whether, but when", "Engineering teams? Not so much". A flat claim with no turn is a weak hook. Concede-then-pivot is the highest-ceiling mechanism for takes.
4. **Precision is the hook.** Unrounded receipts outperform round claims: $23,603.96, 75,000 subs, 1,000 hours, 9 months. The specific number *is* the curiosity. Never round a real figure into a marketing number.
5. **Match the mechanism to the intent, then rotate it.** Teaching → number+stakes or myth-bust. Arguing → bold declaration or concede-then-pivot. Client insight → anonymized story lead. Giveaway → plain value + 👇. There are 16 catalogued mechanisms in the bank; rotate across them (see Hook process), don't lean on the one that worked last week.
6. **Reach and engagement pull different hooks.** Broad/aspirational/curiosity hooks (receipts, "so back", salary-safety, milestones) maximize reach. Opinion/identity hooks ("Most people…", concede-then-pivot, story leads, norm-violation) maximize replies. Decide the lever before the mechanism.
7. **Two lines, colloquial, open loop.** Line 1 = claim/scene, line 2 = turn/promise, whitespace between. Register is casual, not corporate ("so back", "Not so much 😅"). A trailing "…" that suspends the payoff recurs across the biggest hits. Keep the hook to what sits above the "…see more" fold.
8. **Ground every hook in something Shaw would actually say.** A hook must trace to a real line, a real number, or a stance Shaw actually holds. "Delete your reports" tests well as a pattern but misstates his view (he calls reports "powerful" and pitches the level above them) — so it fails. From a transcript, build the hook on a verbatim quote or a real figure, not an invented provocation.

## Hook process (before drafting any post)

**Explore the hook space before committing (never draft body-first).** Before writing a single line of body:

1. Generate **10 candidate hooks in two halves: 5 in Shaw's voice, 5 written as named outside creators.** Shaw's half draws on Part 1 of `references/hook-bank.md`, the borrowed half on Part 2. Within each half use genuinely different mechanisms, not variations of one idea. The split is the point — the borrowed half exists to show Shaw registers he wouldn't reach for on his own, so he can steal the shape without adopting the posture.
2. **Print the source line next to each hook** — the verbatim quote, number, or stance it's built on. If a candidate can't cite a source, it fails Hook Principle 8 and gets cut.
3. **Run the novelty check.** Look at the mechanism *and* hook skeleton of the last 3–4 weeks of posts in the SFC database, and kill any candidate that repeats a recent one. (A fresh idea with a stale hook shape still reads as repetitive — which is why gathering recent-post context first is mandatory.)
4. **Present the survivors as two labeled sections** — "Your voice" and "Other people's voices" — attributing each borrowed hook to the creator whose register it's in. Name mechanisms in plain English ("threat reversal", "concede-then-pivot"), and never print hook-bank ID numbers: they collide with the menu's own numbering and mean nothing outside the reference file. Note which lever each pulls (reach vs engagement), recommend one, and wait for Shaw to pick before drafting the body.

Rotation target: no hook mechanism repeats within ~3–4 weeks.

**Close the edit loop (this is what makes the voice compound).** When Shaw edits or rejects a hook, that delta is the highest-signal training data there is. After he revises a hook or opening, diff his version against the draft, infer the *principle* behind each change (not the one-off wording), and present it back as a short list. Let him confirm which generalize, then feed only those into this skill via `skill-updater`. Over ~10–20 edited hooks, drafts should start where Shaw would normally end. Do this at the end of any session where a hook was rewritten.

## Signature sentence-level moves (from the recent copy)

- **The mid-sentence "however" pivot.** The contrast engine is "however" set off by commas inside the sentence: "AI agents, however, are changing the economics." / "The difference, however, is where that folder lives." Sentence-initial "But", "So", and "Plus," handle lighter turns. Mark the turn explicitly even on the hook: when the second line reverses the first, open it with a contrast marker ("...writes 90%+ of their code with AI. But their velocity hasn't changed...") rather than leaving the contrast implied. "But" or a "however" pivot both work; the point is naming the turn, not the word.
- **The reframe couplet.** Near the close, negate the expected lesson, then state the real one: "The win wasn't a fancy agent. It was capturing one repeatable process." / "The goal isn't a big CLAUDE.md. It's the smallest one that saves you from repeating yourself." (Carried the #2 reach post of June–July 2026.) The negated half names the surface-level win the reader came for; the affirmed half has to name a **durable, concrete outcome they keep** — a captured process, a task off their plate, a smaller file that stops repeat work. A cleverer abstraction in the affirmed slot ("it's judging a skill you're building by the stopwatch on a task you already know") reads as wordplay and lands soft, even when the sentence is well-formed. If the second half can't be pointed at, it isn't the payoff yet. This is the closing case of Principle 1: the couplet is where the post cashes out, so it needs the most concrete noun in the piece, not the smartest one.
- **Argue in notation.** Math and symbols do rhetorical work inside prose: "Access ≠ adoption", "→" for causal chains, inline arithmetic "(20% x 10 = 200%)", casual approximations ("~80% vs ~50%, say 65%"), and single-word ALL CAPS for stress ("you need to DOUBLE", "will NOT come from coding"). Present in 3 of the 5 widest-reach recent posts.
- **The ellipsis open loop.** A trailing "…" on the hook's first or second line suspends the turn: "Here's why that doesn't work…" / "Service businesses are so back…" Appeared on all three of the biggest-reach recent posts.
- **The parenthetical wink.** Parens are emphasis, not just hedging: one intensifier mid-noun-phrase — "a (very) time-consuming process", "a (small) dinner", "a (proper) agent harness", "a CRM that (actually) stayed up to date".
- **Work is physical stuff you move.** "Delete 5–10 hours of busywork", "take that off her plate", "stuff their CLAUDE.md with rules", "pumped the brakes on my SaaS ambitions", "one-shotted by Claude". The default analogy for agents is hiring: intern, new hire, onboarding, "like you would a new hire".
- **Self-posed question pivots.** Mid-body, Shaw interviews himself to advance the argument — "So what does it take to drive behavior change?" — and the next line answers. Distinct from the closing CTA question.

## Explanation moves (carried over from Shaw's live calls)

How Shaw explains things out loud, distilled from ten call transcripts (workshops, sales calls, client check-ins — June–July 2026). These translate directly into written explanations:

- **Count it out loud, then unpack.** Announce the shape before the content: "it comes down to three things", "The way I see it, there are 2 ways to do this...", "It's two different things." The reader gets the container first; then each item gets unpacked in order.
- **Explain through workplace analogies — and flag them.** The master frame is the intern/new hire, extended with first-day-on-the-job detail ("imagine you hire an intern but don't give them access to Jira — they're not going to be as helpful as they could be"). Coin others from everyday work and life, never from technical reference points: a context window = "10 textbooks worth of text", a sandbox = "a computer within your computer", AI overspend = "an ignorance tax". It's fine to tag the device ("to use an analogy").
- **Concrete first, principle after.** Show the real thing — the story, the number, the failure — then name the concept it illustrates. Defer abstraction explicitly rather than front-loading a definition. Even mistakes convert to material: "mistakes are good because it gives us an opportunity to give feedback and iterate."
- **Close every explanation with a punch line.** A long explanatory passage ends in a one-line compression: "That's the punch line." / "It's just faster, better." / "It's just a smarter version of chat." When the punch line names the real lesson by negating the expected one, it's the reframe couplet above — the punch line is the general case.
- **Tiered advice: the proper way vs. the scrappy way.** Advice never comes as one answer. Give two paths ranked by effort, then the rule for choosing: "don't jump to the proper way just because it's the proper way", "go after the lowest hanging fruit first", start small and scrappy, you can always refactor later.
- **Cast the reader into a role and do their math.** Name who the reader is in the story ("If you're the champion for your team...") and run their arithmetic for them: "you spend eight hours a week experimenting so the other three PMs don't have to. That 3xes the return."

## Mechanics (expressions of the principles, not extra rules)

- Emoji lexicon, used sparingly: 👉 CTA · ♻️ repost footer · 😅 self-deprecation · 🥳 celebration · 👇 "content below" · 😉 after a cheeky claim · 🙏 public thanks. Warmth comes from text emoticons :) and :P, not emoji — :) especially at the end of invitations ("come trade lessons and stories :)").
- ✅/❌ pairs for contrast lists (risk vs. benefit).
- No hashtags. None appear in any top post.
- Punctuation stays flat in the post: no em-dashes. Where you'd reach for one, use a period (often plus "But" to open the contrast: "That overrun isn't waste — it's the price" becomes "But that overrun isn't waste. It's the price"), a comma, or parentheses for an aside. Line breaks handle the pacing a dash would; the dash adds a drama this voice doesn't want.
- Trim intensifiers that don't earn their place: "leave it wide open" → "leave it open", "already at $370" → "at $370". The specific noun or number carries the weight; the modifier dilutes it.
- Numbered items formatted as "1. Header" with a blank line before the commentary.
- Titles/hooks may use a number + stakes formula: "10 YouTube channels that keep me ahead of 99% of AI Entrepreneurs", "3 side hustles that made me $23,603.96".

## Quick self-check before delivering a draft

1. Would this hook stop Shaw's actual audience (technical folks leaving 9-5s, AI builders, engineering leaders) — or is it generic creator-speak?
2. Is every claim carrying a real, specific number or name where one exists?
3. Does it read as a peer sharing, or a guru performing? (Look for at least one honest hedge or admission.)
4. Can a skimmer get the skeleton in 2 seconds?
5. Listicle or take — and is the machinery matched to the choice?
6. Does every abstraction get a plain restatement ("In other words..."), and is at least one claim anchored to a dated window of recent experience?
