# Channel: Sales Emails (warm)

1:1 emails to someone who already knows Shaw — a past client, a post-call lead, a referral, a booked-then-ghosted prospect. Cold strangers → `channel-outreach.md`. No conversion intent → `email-helper`.

Lock before drafting: who they are and the context, the single next step, the specific recent history you can reference.

## Channel moves

1. **Short wins.** Three lines beat five. The goal is a reply, not a pre-closed deal — long setup signals uncertainty.
2. **Ask one question.** The whole email can be a question plus signature. Make "no" easy and "yes" specific.
3. **Use the context only a warm email has.** "You mentioned the three hours of reporting every Friday..." If you don't have that, ask why this is a warm email.
4. **Personalize line one — it's the preview text.** "Karen — quick one about the reporting workflow" gets opened; "I wanted to follow up on our previous conversation" gets archived.
5. **Match commitment to relationship.** Post-call: "want me to send the intake?" Past client: "worth a 15-minute catch-up?" Ghosted: "still on your radar, or should I stop bugging you?" — permission to say no often surfaces a yes.
6. **Offer something useful either way.** A Loom they can forward is valuable whether or not they buy.
7. **Sign "— Shaw."** Not "Best regards, Shaw Talebi."

## Structure

Opener using recent context (1 line) → the specific thing you're following up on (1–2 lines) → the ask, answerable in a reply (1 line) → "— Shaw". Longer than that, ask what the extra lines are doing.

## Worked example — post-call follow-up

> Karen — you mentioned the three hours of reporting every Friday is what's killing you. I've been thinking about what that looks like if we tackle it first in the 30-day intensive.
>
> Want me to send a 5-minute Loom walking through what Week 1 would look like specifically for your reporting workflow? No call needed to watch it.
>
> If it lands, you can reply "in" and I'll send the intake.
>
> — Shaw

Uses preview text, references verifiable call context, offers value before the ask, and steps down the commitment (watch → reply) instead of escalating to a call.

## Pricing, discount, and objection replies

The reply's job is to hold value and advance the deal, not to answer literally and stop.

1. **State the price plainly and hold the line.** If they asked for a discount, decline directly ("I won't be able to do a discount"), then pivot to levers that lower *cost* without cutting *price* — fewer seats, a group session, a smaller starting scope.
2. **Recommend the highest-leverage starting point.** Not a flat menu — point at where the ROI is most obvious.
3. **Anchor price to a concrete outcome.** Shaw's go-to: "Most people delegate 5-10 hr/wk of busywork to Claude after the session, which has an outsized impact when the right people apply it."
4. **A pricing question is often the moment to widen scope,** not just size it.
5. **Close soft and open.** "What do you think?" invites the next message; "just say the word" presumes the close.
6. **When the gap is an order of magnitude, hand them off.** No lever bridges a third-of-the-price budget. Name the one thing you can honestly do at their number, then offer an intro to someone in the network at that price point — it keeps the relationship, protects the rate, and banks goodwill with the person you refer.

**Worked example (discount ask):**

> While I won't be able to do a discount, here are a couple of ways we could get the cost down.
>
> 1. Fewer seats: start with 1-3 sessions instead of 5.
> 2. Group session: a single workshop where I walk through the framework and share examples. Priced at $5k, but no limit on seats.
>
> I'd recommend starting with the 1:1 sessions with the exec team. Most people delegate 5-10 hr/wk of busywork to Claude after the session, which has an outsized impact when the right people apply it.
>
> What do you think?
