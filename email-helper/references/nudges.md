# Nudges

A nudge is the message Shaw sends when his last email on a thread got no reply. That's the whole scope: post-call follow-ups, check-in openers, and replies to something they wrote are different emails and live elsewhere.

## Write it from the message it's chasing

A nudge has one job: make replying easier now than it was the first time. What makes it easier depends on what the unanswered message asked, so reread that message before writing a word. A line that could be pasted under any email is the tell that this step got skipped. `Did you get a chance to look at this?` under a message that asked `How did the UK consolidation go?` points at nothing, and the recipient can feel that nobody reread the thread.

Three things hold across every nudge:

- **Short.** One to three lines. The original is one scroll up, so nothing gets re-pitched or re-explained.
- **A new angle each time, measured against everything they've received.** The second nudge on a thread never repeats the first, and the comparison set is wider than the thread: it is every email this person has gotten from Shaw, across threads and templates, because they remember all of it and the drafter remembers none of it. Read sent mail to them before writing. Same line twice reads as a sequence firing, not a person writing (SKILL.md → "The template is the prior; the recipient's memory is the update").
- **Two shapes, never a hybrid.** The *standard* nudge carries a greeting, a bumping line, the ask, and `-Shaw`, each on its own line; it is the default, and what the first and second nudge on a thread almost always are, because the recipient still needs a beat of context before the question. The *tight* nudge is one line, no greeting, `-Shaw` optional; it fits when the question stands entirely on its own in a thread that is warm or near its end: a closer, a "still a good time?" on the day of, a single re-narrowed time. The second nudge is rarely tight, and when it is, it is literally one line, not a shortened standard. What never works is a greeting sitting on top of a bump and an ask fused into one paragraph; that reads as neither shape. Greeting on a standard nudge is optional on the first and drops after that; `-Shaw` stays until the thread gets very casual.

  Standard ([Person] / [Company], 10/23/26, FU1 on a check-in):

  > Hey [First Name],
  >
  > Just following up.
  >
  > Any new use cases worth sharing, or is it still mostly the Monday PMM update?
  >
  > -Shaw

  Tight: the closing lines further down, and `Today at 12PM still a good time for our check-in? -Shaw` ([Person], 10/8/26).

**The cadence picks when and how many; it never picks the words.** The SOPs and the CRM skill say which rung a thread is on (FU1, FU2, the close) and when it fires. That is the whole of their authority: a rung name is a timer, not a template. Reaching for "the FU2 line" or "the closing line" because a table says it's time is how a thread that exists to surface results gets a graceful release instead of the question it was asking, and how someone who already said yes gets closed out. The words come from the two rules below, whatever the rung.

- **If they gave a reason for the silence, ask about the reason.** Someone who said "traveling until X" or "slammed this week" hasn't gone quiet; they've told you why they're quiet. The nudge is one line that picks that up (`Still traveling?`), and it reads as a person keeping track rather than a sequence firing. The closing lines further down are for silence with no stated reason; a stated reason means the thread is alive and the close is premature.
- **Chase the loop that matters, not the one phrased as a question.** When the unanswered email carried two things (a form submitted and a visit asked about, a resource sent and a time floated), nudge on the one that moves the relationship forward even if the other was the explicit question. `Did the form come through?` locks a date; "when can I visit?" is a nicety that can wait for the answer.

## Moves Shaw reaches for

These show his range. Pick by fit and write the line fresh from the thread; don't rotate through them or lift the wording.

**Re-ask the actual question, shorter.** The default when the original asked something specific.

> Still up for the 1:1?

> Any progress on the follow-up classifier skill?

> Just wanted to follow up. Do usage stats for Apollo and HubSpot's AI features exist?

> Just wanted to follow up. Did you get a chance to forward the notes to [Person] and [Person]?

**Narrow to one concrete option.** When the original offered a menu, a link, or an open "sometime," pick for them (formatting in SKILL.md → "Single time inline"). This move needs something to narrow: a message that already named one time and linked the calendar has done the narrowing, so a second time on top of it reads as re-proposing rather than nudging, and the plain bump is the right line ([Person], 10/21/26).

> Just wanted to follow up. Does Thurs (Sep 10) at 11AM CT still work for you?

> Just bumping this up. I'm free Saturday morning if that works for you.

**Lower the cost of a yes.** Take a step off their plate.

> Just wanted to follow up. I can send over a calendar hold and adjust it closer to the date if needed, too :)

> Any progress on the follow-up classifier skill? Happy to hop on a quick call if you're stuck anywhere :)

**Offer two answers, both fine.** Pair the ask with a named "not yet" so replying takes one word and neither answer costs them anything.

> Any wins worth sharing from the rest of the team, or is it still early?

**Acknowledge their bandwidth.** For busy people Shaw has a relationship with. It removes the guilt of the late reply, which is often what's blocking it.

> I know you're super busy. Still happy to do a 30-min check-in whenever the timing's right to see how things are going with Claude :)

> Just bumping this up. Still happy to help with any Claude blockers whenever the timing's right :)

**Name their situation, lightly.** When Shaw knows why someone is probably quiet (a problem they've described, a season they're in), a one-line read on their world with some humor replaces the status question entirely. It shows he's keeping track of them, not the thread, and it gives them something to react to instead of a report to file. Based on a real send ([Person] / [Company], 10/15/26), after two "how's the skill coming along" check-ins had gone unanswered; she replied within five minutes with a win.

> Hey [First Name], I suspect the inbox might still be a disaster, so just bumping this up 😅
>
> Also happy to do a call where we talk through this if that's easier.

**Add something new.** A reason to reply that didn't exist last time: news, a resource, or a specific from their situation. One or two sentences, tied to what they care about. This is the one move that runs longer.

> Just following up on this.
>
> In case it's helpful, I recently became an official Anthropic SMB training partner. Also, here's the recording of a recent workshop I hosted geared toward business leaders if they want to get a sense of how I teach.

> Just wanted to follow up.
>
> Last time we talked, you all were thinking through how to standardize AI across your SDLC. I've recently talked to 2 other firms about this and am happy to share patterns that have worked (and failed) there.
>
> Opposed to a quick chat?

**Ask about their problem, not the meeting.** When the stalled ask was a call, check which of your messages on the thread they did answer. If they reply to questions about their situation and go quiet on scheduling, the call isn't worth their time yet, and another timing ask won't change that. Name the problem they raised in one plain clause, without the detail or their sharper phrasing, and ask where it stands. The call comes back once they've said the problem is still live.

> Before, you mentioned AI costs were going up without clear wins yet.
>
> Has that started to turn, or is it still mostly a cost?

**The plain bump.** `Just wanted to follow up. Did you get a chance to look at this?` is a real Shaw line and the right first nudge on most threads. What moves a nudge off it is conversation to draw on: a lead or client Shaw is nurturing, someone he's gone back and forth with, a person he talked to at an event. There, ground the nudge in what was actually said (a problem they named, what they asked for, where things left off), because that is what makes it read as written to them rather than sent to a list.

The bumping line itself varies too: `Just wanted to follow up.` is the most common, `Just following up on this.` and `Just bumping this up.` show up later in a thread, and plenty of nudges skip it and open on the question.

## The closing nudge

The last nudge before letting a thread go depends on whether they ever opted in.

**They expressed interest** (wrote in, asked for a proposal, said yes to a call, then went quiet): the bare line, nothing else. No greeting, no sign-off.

> Have you given up on this?

It's blunt on purpose. They asked for this, so the question is fair, and it tends to draw a reply where a polite line doesn't.

**They never opted in** (Shaw opened the thread: a check-in, a re-engagement, an offer they didn't ask for): release them and leave the door open, naming the specific thing he'd be glad to talk about.

> Seems like the timing is bad right now.
>
> Either way, feel free to reach out if you ever want to chat about how other firms are rolling out AI across their portfolio companies (or anything else).
>
> -Shaw

The blunt line would be presumptuous here, since there's nothing they committed to that they could have given up on. When part of the offer missed but another part might land, the same shape narrows instead of closing:

> Seems like the team 1:1s aren't a great fit right now. Still happy to talk through the partnership ideas you had in mind :)

When Shaw intends to come back (a client in nurture, a relationship he's keeping warm), the open door is the wrong close: it hands them the next move, and they've already shown they won't make it. Name when he'll return instead, so the thread parks on his clock and the next touch reads as the promised one rather than a fresh chase. Based on a real send ([Person] / [Company], 10/15/26), replacing the standing-offer paragraph after three silent nudges:

> Seems the timing is bad right now. I'll check back in next month :)
>
> -Shaw

**Someone else opened the thread** (an intro: a mutual contact made the connection, Shaw replied with the ask, and the new contact never answered): ask about timing with a question they can say no to.

> Is it a bad time for this?
>
> -Shaw

Neither line above fits here. They never asked for anything, so the blunt line presumes a commitment that isn't there. Releasing them walks away from an introduction a third person vouched for. A no-oriented question keeps the door open and makes the easy reply "no, just busy."

How many nudges a thread gets and when each goes out is the CRM skill's cadence, not this file's.
