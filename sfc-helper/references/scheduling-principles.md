# Scheduling principles

How to lay out a week of posts. These are principles, not rules — they carry their reasoning so you can weigh them against each other when a week doesn't fit the usual shape. Only one number in here is a commitment (five posts a week); everything else is judgment.

Each principle is tagged with where it comes from: **[Shaw's data]** = derived from his own post analytics, **[external]** = from published research (see Evidence notes at the bottom), **[hypothesis]** = believed but untested.

---

## Volume and rhythm

**1. At least five posts a week, up to two a day.** Five is a floor, not a target. The best-controlled external study found no ceiling where posting more starts to hurt per-post performance, so a heavier week is a capacity question rather than a risk. Weekends are legitimate slots for a personal profile — the "never post weekends" advice comes from Company Page data and doesn't transfer. **[external]**

**2. Alternate reach and engagement rather than blocking either.** Engagement on one post makes LinkedIn likelier to surface the next post to those same people, so reach posts warm the pool that later posts land in. This is architecture, not folklore: LinkedIn's 2026 feed model is sequence-aware and its retrieval stage explicitly favors your next post for someone who just engaged with you. Note the implication — the priming works *pairwise*, which is why alternating beats front-loading all the reach posts into the start of the week. **[external]**

**3. Connected posts need at least a day between them; unrelated posts can share a day.** A tee-up needs time to cook — pairing a setup and its payoff on the same day wastes the priming. The corollary handles doubling up: two posts on one day are fine precisely when they *aren't* connected. When two posts do share a day, vary the format and topic; back-to-back posts in the same format measurably suppress each other, and that (not any specific number of hours) is the real constraint. **[external]**

**4. Timing doesn't matter — don't spend attention on it.** Recorded here so it never eats time again. Four large datasets name four mutually incompatible "best hours," and one of them inverted its own answer year-over-year. Day-of-week is worth under 10% for personal profiles. Format and content dominate timing by roughly an order of magnitude. The only real dead zone is midnight–4am local. **[external]**

## What earns a slot

**5. Rank ideas on two dimensions and anchor on the intersection.** Engagement rate and reach come from different generators — audience fit versus contradicting a belief the reader holds. Rank candidates on both separately. Ideas that would place in the top half of *both* are the broad-hook-plus-substance archetype; weight the week toward them. **[Shaw's data]**

**6. Merge thin, split fat.** Judge the page body, not the title. A definition with no meat merges into a neighbor; a page with two distinct central ideas is two posts. Run this lens before scheduling, because it changes how many slots you're actually filling. **[Shaw's data]**

**7. Save the highest-ceiling reach idea for an unclaimed week.** A big swing spends the audience's attention and, if it lands, produces momentum that flows to whatever you post next. On a week already committed to an ask, that momentum has nowhere useful to go — and a genuine tail event needs room to be ridden, which a full campaign calendar doesn't leave. **[Shaw's data]**

**8. Play the hits.** A winner isn't spent. Re-run proven posts as variations, staggered out past the four-week recycle window, so a meaningful share of any month is repurposed proven material. This requires actually maintaining the hit list — a 90-day and a 1-year lookback at top performers, not just the trailing 28 days the weekly report covers. Reach is fat-tailed, so the handful of posts that hit carry more information than everything else combined. **[external, aligned with Shaw's data]**

## Asks

**9. A post either drives a CTA or it doesn't.** No middle, no soft-CTA. CTA posts are occasional, ride momentum that already exists, and get their slots protected — campaign sequences own their days and the backlog fits around them, not through them. Everything else is pure value with no ask attached.

Put the link in the post body. It costs a personal profile roughly 20–27% of reach, but that toll prices correctly here: only CTA posts carry links and CTA posts aren't reach plays anyway. The link-in-first-comment workaround is worse than it used to be — the tests supporting it are from 2021, and the sources that measured it recently find comment links deboosted harder than body links. **[external]**

**10. Tee up anything with a clear ask.** A CTA post shouldn't arrive cold. Build to it in whatever form is natural — a definition before a video share, a client story before an event promo, a thesis before its proof. Only when natural: a forced tee-up reads as a two-part ad, and skipping it is better than manufacturing one.

Hold this one loosely. Priming is well-established in general psychology and in email launches, but nobody has published a test of teaser sequences improving CTA conversion on organic LinkedIn. It's a plausible mechanism with a real architectural story behind it (see #2), but it's currently belief. **[hypothesis]**

**11. Post on the day of your event.** Roughly 17% of webinar registrations happen on the day itself and ~59% land in the final week. A morning-of post is arithmetic, not hype. **[external]**

## Format

**12. Multi-image beats single image beats no image — when natural.** Multi-image is currently the strongest format on both engagement rate and reach at Shaw's follower tier, and the rate lift from having *any* image is large. But images have to earn their place: a graphic that does real work (a framework, a plot, the visual that *is* the point of a tee-up post) helps, and images bolted on to satisfy a quota don't. If half a week's posts carry a natural image, that's a good week. **[external + Shaw's data]**

Two caveats worth knowing. Single-image posts underperform text on reach for accounts above ~50K followers, so this principle is tier-dependent and should be rechecked as the following grows. And the format data is drawn largely from business pages, so treat the exact numbers as directional.

## Process

**13. Read the calendar before recommending.** What's already scheduled, what's blank-paged, what's parked on the backlog Sunday, and what territory is missing entirely. Recommendations built against an empty mental calendar collide with campaigns already in motion.

## Evidence notes

Source quality in the LinkedIn-advice space is poor — much of the 2026 corpus is AI-generated SEO content citing other SEO content, and the most-cited industry report (van der Blom's *Algorithm Insights*) is gated, so figures attributed to it are secondhand and sometimes garbled. The principles above were graded on that basis. What's worth remembering:

- **Strongest evidence:** the sequence-aware feed architecture (LinkedIn Engineering, March 2026, plus their published papers), the near-total irrelevance of posting time, the format rankings, and the ~30-day window after which a post becomes architecturally unreachable to your own network — which independently validates the four-week recycle convention in `sfc-database.md`.
- **Contested:** posting frequency. The one study with within-account controls finds no ceiling; practitioner reports recommend 2–3/week. They disagree because they measure different things (per-post versus per-week, cross-sectional versus within-account).
- **Pure folklore, discard on sight:** any specific "best hour," the "18-hour gap rule" between same-day posts, engagement multipliers like "a comment is worth 15x a like," and same-weekday-regularity claims. None of these trace to a dataset.
- **Untested:** whether teeing up a CTA actually improves its conversion. Testable across repeat launches if it ever seems worth the effort.
