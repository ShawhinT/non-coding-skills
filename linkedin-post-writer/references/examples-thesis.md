# Thesis Examples

Opinion posts, trend analysis, and bigger ideas. Notice the narrative arc — these don't just state a thesis, they tell the story of why it matters. Personal evidence is compressed into proof, not expanded into content.

---

## Example: AI Expanding Human Potential

Here is something I believe (that people will disagree with).

>> AI will expand human potential, not diminish it.

As the tasks that computers can do become more sophisticated, there's a natural fear that they will replace much of the work we (i.e. humans) do, making us obsolete.

However, I don't see that happening.

Today:
- Claude Code writes ~99.9% of the code I generate
- ChatGPT advises me on almost all content, strategy, and product ideas
- My most time-consuming tasks are being automated with Agent Skills

Yet, I'm busier than ever.

Why?

Because AI removes constraints.

Five years ago, building a software product felt like a pipe dream.

Today, I'm building product #3.

But this isn't unique to AI. It's the pattern technology has followed for centuries.

When tools remove constraints, humans don't stop working. We aim higher. We pursue harder problems, explore stranger ideas, and build things that were previously unimaginable.

Nature has no bottom. She will always present new problems to solve and new secrets to reveal.

AI just gives us more leverage to go after them.

## Example: Where Will Humans Go

AI automation is here… So where will humans go?

Over the past few weeks, I've used Claude to automate gathering leads, sales outreach, making email intros, drafting keynote decks, and editing YouTube videos.

This didn't require any fine-tuning or complex AI engineering.

It was all done using MCP, agent skills, and having Claude build itself command line tools.

(And this just feels like the beginning.)

In the face of this rapid automation, a natural question is:

→ If AI can automate most things, what will humans do?

The answer reminds me of a coffee chat I had with David Evans a few months back, who said that the work humans do will shift into the (infinitely-long) tail.

Let me explain.

Work, and its associated value, follows the 80-20 rule, i.e., 80% of the value is generated by 20% of the work (the precise quantities are likely different; the rule is a qualitative one).

This is because work can be described by a Pareto distribution (see the image below), where most work is low-value (on the left-hand side of the plot) and a minority of work is high-value (on the right-hand side).

The rise of agentic AI is expanding the work computers can do, eating up much of the low-value work humans do now.

However, here's the key point.

Even if this AI frontier keeps expanding over the next few years, the tail (i.e. the right part of the plot) is practically infinitely long.

So even if 99% of work gets automated, there is still endless high-value work that humans can (and should) do.

I'm already seeing this personally in my work. Taking tedious tasks like video editing and sales outreach off my plate frees me up to do higher-leverage work like refining my target ICP and the marketing strategies I use to reach them.

Is this a trend you see in your work too?

## Example: AI Fears

AI is scary.

Employees fear losing their jobs.

CEOs/boards fear falling behind (and losing their companies).

While these fears tend to clash, they have the same root cause.

→ Ignorance

And when humans face the unknown, fear is our default.

The solution isn't reassurance.

It's education.

Because once you understand AI clearly, you can see:
- What's possible today vs what's hype
- Where AI amplifies people vs replaces tasks
- What's likely to change in your industry over the next 6–12 months

How are you approaching AI education right now?

## Example: 2 Ways AI Transformations Fail

I recently asked an AI Transformation lead: "Why do transformations fail?"

Here's what they said…

Way 1: Lack of Adoption
Employees are hesitant about AI.
The implicit thought is, "If I become more productive, what does that mean for headcount?"
This leads to some quietly resisting adoption and slowing down progress.

Way 2: Lack of Support
Even if there is bottom-up support, another blocker is a lack of top-down support.
AI might be important to leadership, but just not important right now.
This leads to stalled progress due to a lack of resources and urgency.

How are you seeing AI transformations fail?

## Example: Notion as a System of Record

SaaS companies lost 70% of their valuation due to AI uncertainty
But there's one SaaS I feel getting more valuable with AI.

→ Notion.

Most apps are racing to bolt AI features onto their existing UI (usually a sidebar chatbot).

Notion did something different.

They exposed every action in their product to AI agents.

The result: anything I can do in Notion, Claude can do too.

- Create a page
- Update a database
- Query my CRM
- Add a task to my docket
- Pull engagement notes for a call

But that's not why I think they win.

The deeper value is this:

Notion has become the shared context layer between me and my agents.

When I ask Claude to follow up with a lead, it doesn't need me to re-explain who the lead is. It reads the CRM page.

When I ask it to prep for a call, it pulls the engagement, past notes, and the proposal.

The work I do in Notion becomes the memory my agents use.

This is the playbook for winning in the age of AI.

Don't compete with agents. Become the system of record they rely on.

Every SaaS will eventually face the same question: am I a tool an agent uses, or a layer it bypasses?

Notion answered first.

Which tool in your stack got more valuable when you connected it to AI?

## Example: If AI Is Making You Hopeless (tight single-reframe)

If AI is making you feel despondent...

Read this.

For many people, AI triggers anxiety.

Anxiety about the future, their career, and falling too far behind.

This is compounded by headlines about massive job layoffs and AI news that moves at an overwhelming pace.

So people give up.

They think, "Why should I bother? Either AI or someone using it is just going to replace me."

But that's more anxiety speaking than reality.

The reality of AI is not in news headlines and social media posts.

It's when you actually use tools like Claude and Codex to solve real-world problems that have an impact on your life.

Everything else is noise.

So if you've been feeling hopeless about AI, stop consuming content about it and start using it to learn or build something new!

**Why it works:** ~125 words, one single point (stop consuming, start building). The status quo names the exact fears (the future, their career, falling behind) so the anxious reader feels seen, and that precise naming *is* the validation, so there's no separate "I get it" line. The turn is one flat line ("but that's more anxiety speaking than reality"), not a paragraph explaining the psychology. It names real tools (Claude, Codex) instead of gesturing at "the tools everyone's anxious about." "Everything else is noise." is a flat declarative carrying the whole pivot. The solution stays single (no list of example tactics) and it closes on a directive, not a discussion question.

## Example: Access ≠ adoption (contrast reframe + first-person crux)

Access ≠ adoption.

Most companies' AI rollout plan has been buying Claude seats and hoping for ROI.

However, what people have now realized is that giving people access to tools doesn't make an impact… getting them to change their behavior does.

So what does it take to drive behavior change?

It comes down to the internal cost-benefit analysis people run in their heads.

Is the cost of changing my habits worth the benefit (e.g. time saved, better results)?

The problem here is that the cost is clear (using a new tool or learning a new workflow is immediately painful) while the benefit is not (how do I know this will actually pay off?).

So people fall back on a central heuristic we all use for hard decisions.

What do people like me do?

If no one on my team uses Claude, I probably won't either.
But if someone on my team is getting real results, then I'll give it a proper shot.

This simple feature of human nature writes the whole playbook for driving adoption.

1. Pick a champion.
2. Have them get a personal win with Claude.
3. Have them share that result with the team.
4. Repeat.

While it might start slow, this is how behaviors change at scale.

What's gotten your team to actually adopt Claude?

**Why it works:** The reframe runs on contrast, not explanation — "the cost is clear (immediately painful) while the benefit is not (how do I know this will pay off?)" is two mirrored halves, each with its own parenthetical, instead of a sentence narrating why people hesitate. At the crux it drops into first person ("if no one on *my* team uses Claude, *I* probably won't either"), so the reader runs the heuristic themselves. Note what it *doesn't* do: no manufactured mic-drop couplet at the end — it lands on one quiet line ("this is how behaviors change at scale") and goes to the CTA. It keeps a little connective warmth ("So what does it take to drive behavior change?", "However, what people have now realized…") that a maximal-compression pass would wrongly strip. The CTA names Claude specifically, not "a new tool." The 4-step framework is the payoff and is left to stand on its own.
